Daily DSE Wrap | Monday, 26th October 2026

TZS 14 Billion Day — but Two Block Trades Were Three-Quarters of It, and Foreign Investors Sold 85% of Everything Traded

Monday was the Dar es Salaam Stock Exchange's biggest session in recent weeks by turnover — TZS 14.03 billion in equities, up 260% on Friday — yet the headline flatters the day. Two large pre-arranged block trades supplied 76.5% of that turnover: 3.5 million NMB shares and 316,391 TCC shares, together worth about TZS 10.73 billion. The normal trading board, where everyday investors buy and sell, traded only about TZS 3.30 billion — an ordinary session. And behind the blocks sits the week's real story: foreign investors sold TZS 11.94 billion of shares — 85% of the day's equity turnover — while buying almost nothing (TZS 2.92 million). The two headline indices fell less than 1% each despite that exit. For the everyday investor, Monday was a lesson in reading past the turnover headline: the market absorbed its largest foreign exit of the recent series without a rout, and that absorption — not the TZS 14 billion figure — is the signal worth keeping.

The two headline indices fell together this time, but only modestly — DSEI by 0.83%, TSI by 0.89%. The gap between total market value (down TZS 84.34 billion) and domestic market value (down just TZS 2.40 billion) is explained by the cross-listed companies: KCB fell 1.40% on a TZS 6.30 trillion market value, and EABL's reference price was trimmed by the exchange without trading. When the headline and domestic figures disagree this sharply, follow the domestic one — it tells you what Tanzanian companies actually did.

Foreign participation was almost entirely one-way: foreign buying of TZS 2.92 million against foreign selling of TZS 11,938.38 million — a net outflow of about TZS 11.94 billion in a single session, more than fourteen times Thursday's net outflow. Yet the domestic index fell under 1%. When record selling fails to push prices down, it tells you domestic demand is deep — one of the healthiest signals a frontier market can send, even on a day when the selling itself deserves attention.

3. In Focus: What a Block Trade Tells You — and When It Doesn't Add Up

Most investors watch the prices on the normal trading board — but some of the market's most honest information arrives in the block trade report. A block trade is a large pre-arranged transaction between two investors, negotiated privately and then reported to the exchange. The exchange does not publish the block price, but the report gives you enough figures to work it out: the total value of the counter's trading, minus the normal board's volume multiplied by its average price, leaves the block's value. Divide by the block's shares, and you have the implied price.

Monday's two blocks show both sides of this exercise. The TCC block of 316,391 shares implies a price of roughly 14,000 shillings — dead on the day's average market price of 14,000. That is a routine institutional transfer: someone sold, someone bought, at a fair price. The NMB block of 3,500,000 shares, though, implies roughly 1,800 shillings — about 6% below the day's average market price of 1,920. A discount that large on 3.5 million shares is unusual: either a seller accepted a real price concession to move that much stock at once, or the report carries a quirk. Either way, the honest reading is different from TCC's — this block looks like an exit at a concession, not accumulation, and it should not be read as support for the price. The practical lesson: the implied-price arithmetic is worth doing precisely because it sometimes refuses to reconcile — and a block that doesn't reconcile is itself information.

4. Bond Market: TZS 37 Billion Day as Short-Term Money Piles In

Government bond turnover surged from Friday's quiet TZS 3.06 billion to TZS 37.09 billion in face value across 42 deals (government bonds: face value TZS 36.99 billion, transaction value TZS 40.46 billion; corporate bonds: TZS 91.00 million). The session's weight was at the short end: the 2-year bond at 9.50% interest moved TZS 20.00 billion in face value at about 105% of par (yields 7.5–7.8%) — short-term money is deep and cheap. The brand-new 25-year bond issued on 16 October, now in its second week as the market's long-term benchmark, traded TZS 5.86 billion in 19 deals at prices between 118% and 126% of face value, with yields settling around 10.5–10.7%. When a new bond trades actively for a second straight week and its yield stops wandering, it means the market has accepted it as the reference for long-term Tanzanian government debt. The session's other large print was the 5-year bond at 13.00% interest: TZS 10.00 billion at 111.41% of face value (yield about 11.0%). On the corporate side, trading was small — TZS 91 million in total — led by the ITRUST 4-year bond at 100% of face value (yield about 14.0%). The message for savers: high-quality, tax-free income of around 10–11% a year remains available for those willing to lend to the government for the long term, while short-term money earns around 7.5%.

5. Strategic Outlook: What to Anticipate

Near-Term (this week):

  • Watch whether Monday's foreign selling continues or was a one-day exit: the blocks are done, but the normal board will show whether domestic buyers keep absorbing supply.
  • NMB closed at 1,920 with a heavy queue of sellers above the price and no waiting buyers shown at the close — the overhang above the price is the counter's near-term story.
  • VODA held flat at 1,250 with buyers queuing below the price — a second flat session after the week's falls, and the first sign the slide may have exhausted itself.
  • CRDB closed at 2,870 with sellers now queuing above the price — a change from Thursday's buyer-heavy board, worth watching.

Medium-Term (Q4 2026 — the fourth quarter of 2026):

  • The new 25-year government bond is now a settled, heavily-traded benchmark — its yield, near 10.5%, becomes the reference rate against which every long-term investment in Tanzania is measured.
  • Dividend season positioning typically begins in the final quarter — high-yield counters like TBL (7.68% net) and TCC (7.12% net) tend to attract attention.

Long-Term:

A market that absorbs a TZS 11.94 billion foreign exit in a single session while its domestic index falls under 1% is a market standing on its own feet. Add a functioning long-term bond benchmark being priced in the open market, and you have the two pillars of a maturing capital market: equity depth and a real yield curve. For the patient, long-term investor, that self-reliance is the most constructive backdrop possible — even when the exit itself is the headline. ---

7. Considerations for a Multi-Year Framework

Monday's session will be remembered as the day the blocks told the story — and the day the arithmetic refused to flatter it. The numbers on the normal board were ordinary, the headline turnover was extraordinary, and the gap between them was two pre-arranged trades worth three-quarters of the market. A market where domestic buyers absorb a record foreign exit with barely a 1% fall, where a brand-new 25-year bond settles into its role as the long-term benchmark, and where one block reconciles to the shilling while the other concedes 6%, is a market in the middle of a genuine deepening.
